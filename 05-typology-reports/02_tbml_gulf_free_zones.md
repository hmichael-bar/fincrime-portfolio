## Executive summary

Trade-based money laundering (TBML) moves value by **misrepresenting trade**: the price, quantity, quality or very existence of goods. The FATF and Egmont Group call it one of the main laundering channels. It is hard to detect because the bank sees only the payment, while the goods move through customs systems the bank cannot see [1][2].

Gulf free zones are attractive for TBML and sanctions evasion for the same reasons they attract legitimate business. They offer fast company formation, 100% foreign ownership, light customs touch on re-exports, deep logistics hubs (Jebel Ali, Dubai Airport Free Zone and dozens of others), and banks with correspondent access to USD and EUR. After 2022 the UAE became, in the US government's words, a frequent **transshipment point** for export-controlled goods bound for Russia [3][4]. It is also a long-standing location for **front companies** in Iran's "shadow banking" system [5].

**Bottom line:** For trade customers in or dealing through Gulf free zones, a bank should test **four consistencies**. Does the price fit the market? Does the product fit the customer's business? Does the route fit the goods? Does the payer fit the buyer? TBML and transshipment schemes almost always break at least one.

## 1. How the schemes work

### 1.1 Classic TBML techniques (FATF/Egmont)

| Technique | How value moves | What the bank sees |
|---|---|---|
| **Over-invoicing** | Importer pays more than the goods are worth, so the excess moves value abroad | Payment out looks normal, but the unit price is far above market |
| **Under-invoicing** | Exporter is paid less on paper, and the buyer settles the difference off-book | Low-value invoices, with offsetting payments from unrelated third parties |
| **Multiple invoicing** | Same shipment invoiced and paid several times, often through different banks | Repeat payments with similar descriptions, amounts and counterparties |
| **Phantom shipments** | No goods move at all | No bill of lading or tracking, vague descriptions, freight forwarder unknown |
| **Misdescription** | Goods described as cheaper or controlled goods as uncontrolled (wrong HS code) | HS code or description does not fit price, weight or customer |
| **Third-party payments** | Payment comes from or goes to someone not party to the trade | Payer/beneficiary not on invoice; payments from exchange houses or unrelated companies |

The FATF/Egmont *Risk Indicators* (March 2021) group red flags into structural, trade-activity, documentary, account and transaction categories, and are the baseline for bank controls [2].

### 1.2 Front companies and Iran's "shadow banking"

Iran cannot easily access the international banking system. It settles trade through **exchange houses in Iran** that control networks of **front companies abroad**, especially in the UAE and Hong Kong. Those companies hold bank accounts, receive payment for Iranian exports (often oil), pay for Iran's imports, and **net** the flows against each other. The result is that little money crosses Iran's border directly.

On **6 June 2025** OFAC designated more than 30 people and entities in the **Zarringhalam** network. It said the brothers' Iran-based exchange houses used front companies, including **five UAE-based firms** and **11 Hong Kong firms**, to move billions for Iran's regime. OFAC described falsified invoices and "parallel" settlement as core techniques [5]. To a correspondent bank these look like ordinary general-trading companies.

### 1.3 Dual-use transshipment to Russia

Since 2022, Western export controls (the BIS **Common High Priority List** of items found in Russian weapons, such as microelectronics and CNC tools) have pushed Russian procurement through third countries. The pattern: a **front or newly formed trading company** in a third country orders controlled items from US, EU or Asian distributors. It re-exports them to Russia, often via several logistics companies, and gets paid through a bank in yet another country.

- FinCEN and BIS issued joint alerts on **28 June 2022** and **19 May 2023** (FIN-2023-Alert004). The 2023 supplement added nine priority HS codes and new red flags, e.g. a customer **incorporated after 24 February 2022** trading those HS codes, or a **logistics company or mail centre listed as ultimate consignee** [3].
- On **12 December 2023** OFAC's action against more than 150 targets named UAE free-zone firms (e.g. *365 Days Freight Services FZCO*, *Alfa Logistics FZCO*, *Hyperel FZCO*) for shipping high-priority goods to Russia [4].

### 1.4 Why the Gulf, and what's changed

- **UAE:** The FATF put the UAE under increased monitoring ("grey list") on **4 March 2022** and removed it on **23 February 2024**, citing reforms such as a dedicated executive office for AML/CFT and a financial-crimes court [6]. Western officials continued to press the UAE on free-zone oversight and Russia-related re-exports through 2024 [7]. Removal from the grey list lowers *country* risk, but **free-zone counterparty risk** must still be assessed company by company.
- **Qatar:** The FATF/MENAFATF mutual evaluation of Qatar was adopted in **2023**. It found a strong legal and institutional framework but called for more risk-focused money-laundering prosecutions and improvements in international cooperation [8]. Public enforcement cases routing sanctions evasion through **Qatar's** free zones are far fewer than for the UAE. An analyst should therefore treat Qatar exposure on its own facts, applying the same TBML tests, rather than assume UAE-level transshipment risk.
- **Cyprus link:** Cyprus also appears in these chains. In the *Arthur Petrov* case (Section 3), a **Cyprus shell company** bought controlled US microelectronics for a Russian military supplier. The May 2023 FinCEN/BIS alert describes avionics transshipped **through Armenia and Cyprus** to Russia [3][9]. This mirrors Project 02's finding that Cyprus and UAE corporate-service providers work as a pair. RUSI's research on third-country evasion likewise examines the UAE, Cyprus and Malta among the jurisdictions involved in Russia-related sanctions evasion [11].

## 2. Red flags for banks

**Customer profile**

- A trading company **incorporated after Feb 2022** in a free zone whose business is in HS codes on the Common High Priority List, or whose activity in those codes **jumped** after Feb 2022 [3].
- A "general trading" licence with **no website, staff or warehouse**, at a **mass-registration or flexi-desk address**. Directors or shareholders serve many companies.
- Ownership **changes soon after onboarding**, or nominees and corporate shareholders in other secrecy jurisdictions.

**Trade documents and pricing**

- The **unit price diverges sharply** from market or customs reference prices. FinCEN/BIS list significant overpayment as a red flag [3].
- **Goods don't fit the business** (a textile trader buying FPGA chips), or **routing doesn't fit the goods** (electronics from the US to Dubai and on to Kyrgyzstan or Armenia, then Russia).
- The **ultimate consignee is a freight forwarder, mail centre or financial institution** [3]. Shipping documents are missing, vague or reused.

**Payments**

- **Third-party payments**: buyer and payer differ, payments come from exchange houses or unrelated companies, or there is circular flow between related trading firms [2][5].
- **Many small payments from one foreign account** to multiple similar dual-use suppliers [3].
- Payment instructions **name or resemble** a listed party or its address [3].

## 3. Case examples

**Case 1: Zarringhalam shadow-banking network (OFAC, June 2025).** Iran-based exchange houses (GCM Exchange, Berelian Exchange and others) used UAE and Hong Kong front companies, such as *Wide Vision General Trading L.L.C* and *J.S Serenity FZE*, to settle Iranian trade outside the formal banking system. They hid payments with falsified invoices [5]. *Lesson:* "general trading LLC/FZE" plus payments that net in and out between related firms is a classic shadow-banking profile.

**Case 2: UAE freight forwarders and the December 2023 designations.** OFAC designated free-zone logistics companies for shipping high-priority dual-use goods (e.g. data-transmission equipment) to Russia [4]. *Lesson:* logistics companies are not passive. When a freight forwarder is the consignee or payer, ask who the end user is.

**Case 3: *Arthur Petrov* and a Cyprus shell (US DOJ, 2023–2024).** A Russian-German national was arrested in Cyprus in August 2023 and extradited to the US in August 2024. Prosecutors allege he used a Cyprus company, *Astrafteros Technokosmos*, to buy export-controlled microelectronics from US distributors for a St Petersburg supplier to Russian weapons makers. They say he told vendors the goods were for fire-security systems in Cyprus [9]. These are allegations; check the docket for outcome. *Lesson:* a stated end-use that doesn't fit the product (military-grade microcontrollers for fire alarms) is the red flag.

**Case 4: The broader laundering market.** FinCEN's August 2025 advisory on Chinese money-laundering networks (FIN-2025-A003) shows how TBML and mirror trades let cartels and other criminals swap value across borders. It asks filers to check the "trade-based money laundering / black market peso exchange" box on SARs where relevant [10]. *Lesson:* the same netting mechanics serve sanctions evasion and drug laundering, so TBML controls protect against both.

## 4. Detection and screening recommendations

1. **Trade-price analytics.** Compare invoice unit prices against market or customs reference data by HS code, and alert on extreme deviations (e.g. more than 50% from the reference). This is the single most effective TBML control and needs document capture in trade finance.
2. **HS-code-aware onboarding.** Ask every trading customer for its main HS codes. Apply EDD automatically when they intersect the Common High Priority List or other dual-use lists, and re-ask annually [3].
3. **Free-zone counterparty due diligence.** Verify licence, physical presence, ownership and incorporation date against the free-zone registry. Treat flexi-desk or mass addresses and post-2022 incorporation as risk factors, not automatic exits (FinCEN/BIS warn against wholesale de-risking) [3].
4. **End-use and end-user questions.** For dual-use goods, request end-user certificates and route details. Escalate when the consignee is a logistics company or the route touches known transshipment hubs (Armenia, Kyrgyzstan, Kazakhstan, Türkiye, UAE) [3][4].
5. **Third-party and netting detection.** Monitor for payer ≠ buyer, exchange-house funding and circular flows among related trading companies. Use network analysis (Project 02 methods) to link "unrelated" counterparties through shared directors or addresses [5].
6. **Correspondent banking.** Ask respondent banks in the Gulf about their free-zone customer controls and Russia/Iran exposure. Review nested activity in respondents' payment flows.
7. **SAR practice.** Use FinCEN key terms where they apply (e.g. "FIN-2022-RUSSIABIS" for the 2022 joint alert). Describe the inconsistency precisely: which price, product, route or payer did not fit.
